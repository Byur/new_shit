## 1. THE 1950 REPLACEMENT BRIDGE ("Sturdy Gertie")

### 1.1 Principal dimensions

The most authoritative source is the primary document **[Chas. E. Andrew, *Final Report on Tacoma Narrows Bridge*, Washington Toll Bridge Authority, June 1952](https://digitalarchives.wa.gov/ViewRecordMedia.aspx?RID=47D64004ADAAFB98D0BB2D799C7894D1)** (PDF, 63 pp.; hereinafter **WTBA Final Report**), whose "IMPORTANT DIMENSIONS" appendix reads verbatim:

> Total structure length — **5,979 feet**
> Suspension bridge — **5,000 feet**
> Center suspension span — **2,800 feet**
> Shore suspension spans, each — **1,100 feet**
> East approach and anchorage — **365 feet**
> West approach and anchorage — **614 feet**
> Center span height above water — **187.5 feet**
> Width of roadway — **49 ft. 10 in.**
> Width of sidewalks (2), each — **3 ft. 10 in.**
> Diameter of suspension cables — **20¼ inches**
> Weight of suspension cables — **5,441 tons**
> Total length of single wire — **20,000 miles**
> Suspended weight sustained by cables (in excess of) — **18,160 tons**
> Number of No. 6 wires, each cable — **8,705**
> Shore anchors for cables, weight — **66,000 tons**
> TOWERS: Height above piers — **467 feet**; Weight of each tower — **2,675 tons**

The same dimensions appear (independently restated) in the **[National Register nomination for the 1950 bridge](https://cms.cityoftacoma.org/planning/historic-preservation/NominationDocs/Narrows1950bridge.pdf)** (`cms.cityoftacoma.org`, retrieved): total structure length 5,979 ft; suspension bridge 5,000 ft; center span 2,800 ft; shore spans 1,100 ft each; roadway 49 ft 10 in; sidewalks 3 ft 10 in each; cable diameter 20¼ in; cable weight 5,441 tons; suspended weight 18,160 tons; 8,705 No. 6 wires per cable; anchor weight 66,000 tons; tower height above piers 467 ft; tower weight 2,675 tons. It adds: *"The suspension cables are 60 feet center to center. The sides or stiffening trusses are **33 feet in depth** and, at the time of construction, were deeper than those on any other known suspension bridge."*

**Simpler figures from [Wikipedia, *Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1950)*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacoma_Narrows_Bridge_(1950))** (retrieved; article carries a "needs more citations" banner): total length 5,979 ft (1,822.4 m); longest span 2,800 ft (853.4 m), side spans 1,100 ft (335.3 m); opened **October 14, 1950**; "Sturdy Gertie." It also gives the comparison: towers **"rose 58 feet (18 m) higher and 21 feet (6.4 m) wider"** than Gertie's; cables **20¼ in (510 mm)** vs Gertie's **17½ in (440 mm)**; anchor blocks supporting a load **"1.6 times as much as the original bridge."**

- **Opening date: October 14, 1950.** Confirmed by WTBA Final Report, Wikipedia, the NRHP nomination ("opened to traffic on October 14, 1950"), and the **[AAPT DVD user's guide](https://aapt.org/Store/upload/tacoma_narrows2.pdf)**: *"opened on October 14, 1950."* (The NRHP nomination adds that *"all components of the structure were finally in place by November 1951."*)
- **Main span:** 2,800 ft (853.4 m).
- **Total length:** 5,979 ft (1,822.4 m). The 1940 bridge was **5,939 ft** — i.e. the replacement is 40 ft longer ([Wikipedia, *Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940)*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacoma_Narrows_Bridge_(1940)); the FWA report gives "5939 ft.").
- **Side spans:** two, each 1,100 ft (335.3 m).
- **Deck/roadway width:** **49 ft 10 in** roadway per the primary source and NRHP nomination. **Conflict:** Wikipedia (1950) says the roadway was **46 ft (14 m)** wide. The two are not reconciled by any source I found.
- **Tower height:** **467 ft above piers** (WTBA Final Report appendix; NRHP nomination). The WTBA body text separately calls the free-standing east tower *"its full height of **462 feet**"*, and describes a cable saddle blocked *"some **510 feet above the water level**."* The AAPT user's guide says the towers *"increased in height by 59 feet to **507 feet**."* **Sources disagree**; all four figures are given as stated.
- **Stiffening truss depth:** **33 ft** (10 m). WTBA Final Report: *"the stiffening trusses are on 60 foot centers with a depth of **33 feet center to center of chords**."* It further states the trusses had *"an average moment of inertia of **6,890,000 ins.⁴** as compared to **120,400 ins.⁴** for the original bridge"* (~57×). Floorbeams were trussed type, 11 ft deep, with a **double lateral system** of bracing in the planes of both top and bottom chords.
- **Main cable diameter:** **20¼ in** in the WTBA dimensions appendix, the NRHP nomination and Wikipedia — **but the WTBA body text computes "twenty and one-eighth (20⅛) inches."** The 1952 primary document therefore disagrees with itself. For the 1940 bridge the **[FWA board report](https://babel.hathitrust.org/cgi/ssd?id=mdp.39015021064251)** says *"The diameter of each cable under wrapping is **17⅛ ins.**"*, whereas Wikipedia (1950) says Gertie's cable was *"17½ inches."* Report both.
- **Weight vs the 1940 bridge:** **1.6× (160 %)** per Wikipedia (1950) and the NRHP nomination, which says *"while weighing 160 percent more than the old structure, the new bridge's superstructure design only increased the dead load pressure on the piers by 6 percent."* The WTBA Final Report corroborates the 6 % figure: *"the actual dead load foundation pressures were increased only approximately six per cent by the added weight of the new superstructure"* and *"does not exceed 15,300 lbs./sq. ft."*
- **Other verified specs (WTBA Final Report body):** main cables = **19 strands of 458 No. 6 gage wires**, area **252 sq. in.**; sag ratio **1:10** for the main span vs **1:12** for the former bridge; lightweight-aggregate concrete specified at "not to exceed **120 pounds per cubic foot**," actual mix about **118 lbs/cu ft**; the bridge withstood winds *"as high as **75 miles per hour**"* in January–March 1951 *"without any visible sign of vertical or torsional oscillation."*

### 1.3 DECK TYPE — CRITICAL VERIFICATION

**1950 bridge = OPEN GRATING / wind slots. VERIFIED, primary source.**

WTBA Final Report, verbatim:

> "An entirely new innovation in suspension bridge design was introduced through the use of **open gratings as longitudinal slots at curb lines and between roadway lanes**. The **curb slots are nineteen inches in width** and the **roadway slots are thirty-three inches wide** separating the **four concrete slab traffic lanes of nine foot width**. The slots were designed to be fabricated from **five-inch I-Beam-Lok open floor grating with six-inch channel edges**."

The same report: *"Extensive studies were made of various slotted deck designs in the wind tunnel at the University of Washington… such a deck possessed inherent characteristics of great value in stabilizing the structure… A completely open-grated deck for such a long structure was considered objectionable."* The **1940 Wikipedia article** ("open strips of wind grating placed in the roadway" / 1950 article: *"open-air stiffening truss"*), the **[NRHP nomination](https://cms.cityoftacoma.org/planning/historic-preservation/NominationDocs/Narrows1950bridge.pdf)** (*"Open steel grid slots were installed between each of the four traffic lanes and at both curbs… a feature unique to this bridge"*), and **[National Geographic](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/tacoma-narrows-bridge-collapse-artificial-reef)** (*"With heavier cables and **open wind grates**, it was stronger—and more stable—than its predecessor"*) all confirm.

**1940 bridge = SOLID. VERIFIED.** The FWA report describes *"solid plate girders **8 ft. ½ in. deep** b. to b. of flange angles"*; *"Each stiffening girder consisted of a web **96 in. × ½ in.**, 4 angles 8 in. × 6 in. × ½ in. and 2 covers 20 in. × ½ in."*; *"Each consists of a plate girder **eight ft. deep**."* The WTBA Final Report: Moisseiff substituted *"a shallow plate girder, **eight feet in depth**, for the stiffening of the superstructure instead of the deep, open truss contemplated by the State."* The 1940 FWA-reported roadway was **26 ft wide** with two 4 ft 9 in sidewalks, **39 ft center-to-center of cables and stiffening girders** — note this last figure is a *cable spacing*, not the roadway width, and Wikipedia's "just 39 feet across" conflates the two.

## 2. THE BRONX–WHITESTONE BRIDGE RETROFIT (the comparable case)

Baseline facts, from **[Wikipedia, *Bronx–Whitestone Bridge*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bronx%E2%80%93Whitestone_Bridge)** (a Good Article; retrieved): designed by **Othmar Ammann** and design engineer **Allston Dana**; **opened to traffic with four lanes on April 29, 1939**; cost $17.5 million; main span **2,300 ft (700 m)**; side spans 735 ft; total (anchorage to anchorage) **3,770 ft**; deck width between cables **74 ft**; towers **377 ft above mean high water**. **Verified:** *"Unlike other suspension bridges, the Bronx–Whitestone Bridge originally did not have a stiffening truss system. Instead, **11-foot (3.4 m) I-beam girders** gave the bridge an Art Deco streamlined appearance."*

### 2.1 Stiffening trusses — WHY, HOW DEEP, WHEN

- **Why:** *"After the 1940 collapse of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge, a bridge of similar design, trusses were added on the Bronx–Whitestone Bridge to minimize the span's oscillations."* The Tacoma bridge *"had employed an 8-foot-deep girder system, much like the 11-foot I-beam girders of the Bronx–Whitestone Bridge."*
- **Depth:** *"On both sides of the deck, **14-foot-high (4.3 m)** steel trusses were installed to weigh down and stiffen the bridge in an effort to reduce oscillation."*
- **When — SOURCES DISAGREE, and the common claim is contestable:**
  - **"1943" version:** [Wikipedia, *Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940)*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacoma_Narrows_Bridge_(1940)) states flatly: *"**Fourteen-foot-high (4.3 m) steel trusses were installed on both sides of the deck in 1943** to weigh down and stiffen the bridge in an effort to reduce oscillation."* This matches the widespread "1943 trusses" claim.
  - **"1947" version (better documented):** the Bronx–Whitestone article's own *Truss installation* section says **$1 million was initially allocated but "construction was deferred due to material and labor shortages during World War II"** (citing a **May 27, 1943** Brooklyn Daily Eagle item about the deferral), that **"Planning for the project resumed in September 1945 at the end of the war"**, that a low bid was announced in **October 1945** at **$1,312,225**, and that **"The stiffening project was completed in 1947."** The four lanes of traffic were widened to six lanes during the same project, the two extra lanes replacing the pedestrian walkways. The **[New York Times, 24 October 1945](https://www.nytimes.com/1945/10/24/archives/bid-on-bridge-job-10-over-42-level-widening-stiffening-painting-of.html)**, headline cited by that article, reads: *"Bid On Bridge Job 10% Over '42 Level; Widening, Stiffening, Painting of Whitestone Span Can Be Done for $1,312,225."*
  - **Assessment:** the trusses were *authorised/allocated* around 1941–1943 but the *installation* was deferred by WWII and **completed in 1947**. Any source that says simply "1943" is at best describing the authorisation, not the installation. **The 1943 date is not safely correct.**
- **Also relevant (1940–41):** immediately after the Tacoma collapse Ammann and Moses first added **cable stays** (eight stay cables, two on each side of both towers, proposed/announced **December 1940**), not trusses — Moses publicly *"denie[d] Tacoma Crash Caused Change."* A Princeton professor's model found the Whitestone main span *"could still oscillate in as many as three segments."*

### 2.2 Removal of the trusses and addition of aerodynamic fairings

- **Trusses REMOVED: 2003.** *"In 2003, the MTA restored the classic lines of the bridge by removing the stiffening trusses and installing fiberglass fairing along both sides of the road deck."* (Bronx–Whitestone article, citing the NYT of 12 Oct 2003 and MTA/Daily News.) The 1940 Tacoma article states the same: *"In 2003, the stiffening trusses were removed and aerodynamic fiberglass fairings were installed along both sides of the road deck."*
- **Fairings:** lightweight, **triangular** fibreglass fairings giving an aerodynamic profile so crosswinds flow through rather than hit the structure. The renovation is dated **2003–2005** by the Bronx–Whitestone article's lead and infobox; the deck was then replaced with a steel **orthotropic deck** (2005–2007), and **mass dampers** were added.
- **Quantified result:** removal of the trusses and other decking changes **reduced the bridge's weight by 6,000 tons, "accounting for some 25% of the mass suspended by the cables"**, and the bridge could then **withstand crosswinds of up to 150 mph, "whereas the trusses could resist crosswinds of no more than 50 mph."**
- **Verdict on the common claim:** *"1943 trusses, 2003 fairings"* — the **2003 fairings are correct**; the **1943 truss date is doubtful** (installation completed 1947 per the best-documented account; the 1943 date reflects authorisation/deferral).

## 3. THE BIRTH OF WIND-TUNNEL TESTING FOR LONG-SPAN BRIDGES

### 3.1 F. B. (Frederick Burt) Farquharson and the model scales

- **1940 emergency programme.** [Wikipedia, *Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940)*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacoma_Narrows_Bridge_(1940)): *"Farquharson and his students built a **1:200-scale model of the bridge** and a **1:20-scale model of a section of the deck**. The first studies concluded on November 2, 1940—five days before the bridge collapse on November 7."* — **Both scale ratios as claimed (1:200 full bridge; 1:20 deck section) are stated here.** I could not find a second, independent, retrievable source that confirms these two exact ratios; they rest on this Wikipedia article. (Wikipedia elsewhere calls him "Frederick Burt Farquharson"; the Vice article calls him "F. Bert Farquharson" — the middle name is given inconsistently across sources.)
- **The redesign model — 1:50, and this is the one the primary source confirms.** WTBA Final Report: *"A wind tunnel sufficiently large to test a **1 to 50 scale model** was designed and built at the University of Washington. As bridge designs were developed they were tested in the tunnel."* The **AAPT user's guide** likewise: *"Studies at the University of Washington Engineering Experiment Station using a **dynamic 1:50 model**, coupled with extensive mathematical analysis, resulted in a design for the new bridge that used deep stiffening trusses instead of the 8-feet girders of the original bridge."* The **NRHP nomination** agrees: *"A **1:50 three-dimensional model** of the original bridge was built to prove that wind velocities acting on models scaled to match the bridge's form and elastic properties would create the same motions as those actually measured and recorded in the field on the prototype."*
- **Reconciliation:** these are **different models for different jobs**, not a contradiction. The **1:200** (whole bridge) and **1:20** (deck section) models were the *1940 diagnostic* models used to study Gertie's behaviour before and after the collapse; the **1:50 dynamic model** was the *redesign* model used from late 1941 for the 1950 structure. The AAPT guide's 1:50 figure is explicitly about *the redesign*: *"the towers increased in height by 59 feet to 507 feet. Studies … using a dynamic 1:50 model … resulted in a design for the new bridge…"* The FWA report separately notes that von Kármán's own wind-tunnel work used a **1:80 scale model of the roadway** at Caltech (*"conducted on a 1:80 model of the roadway"*, Appendix VIII).

### 3.2 The specially built University of Washington wind tunnel

- **Built when / why:** the decision followed the 1940 collapse and the Consulting Board's first meeting *"in the fall of 1941"* (WTBA Final Report). **By 1943** Farquharson was at work in the specially designed tunnel laboratory: Wikipedia (1950) — *"By 1943, he was working in a specially designed wind tunnel laboratory built on the University of Washington campus in Seattle."* Testing of new designs ran **November 1943 through 1945**. WTBA: *"The design of the bridge and original research transpired wholly within the period of the second World War and were greatly handicapped… with the result that approximately **four years** were spent in the process."*
- **Size / capacity:** Wikipedia (1950): the facility *"was large enough to house a scale model of the completed bridge **as long as 100 feet (30 m)**"*, plus section models. The WTBA primary source states its governing limit functionally rather than dimensionally: *"sufficiently large to test a **1 to 50 scale model**."* I found **no** retrieved source that gives the tunnel's physical cross-section; the **"100-foot / 30 m model"** figure is single-sourced to Wikipedia (citing WSDOT, unreachable).
- **Cost and sponsorship:** WTBA Final Report — *"The cost of model tests was financed jointly by the Washington Toll Bridge Authority and the **Public Roads Administration**; each public body assuming approximately one-half the cost which amounted to **$88,341.37**."* Some check tests were made at **Caltech, Pasadena**.
- **Publications:** the results appeared as *Aerodynamic Stability of Suspension Bridges with Special Reference to the Tacoma Narrows Bridge*, University of Washington Engineering Experiment Station **Bulletin 116, Parts I–V (1949–54)** — cited in the AAPT guide's bibliography and by Wikipedia (1940).

### 3.3 The Federal Works Agency inquiry board, 1941

**Exact title and date — verified from the report's own title page** (retrieved full text via HathiTrust):

> **THE FAILURE OF THE TACOMA NARROWS BRIDGE**
> A Report to the Honorable John M. Carmody, Administrator, Federal Works Agency, Washington, D. C.
> **MARCH 28, 1941**
> BOARD OF ENGINEERS: **Othmar H. Ammann · Theodore von Karman · Glenn B. Woodruff**

Source: **[HathiTrust full-view text of the report (id mdp.39015021064251)](https://babel.hathitrust.org/cgi/ssd?id=mdp.39015021064251)**. A PDF copy exists at **[authors.caltech.edu/45680/1](https://authors.caltech.edu/45680/1/The%20Failure%20of%20the%20Tacoma%20Narrows%20Bridge.pdf)** but **this URL was not reachable from this machine** (connection failure on repeated attempts). The reported title, date and the three names are therefore **CONFIRMED**.

Board composition detail from the report's introduction: *"Othmar H. Ammann, Consulting Engineer, of New York City; Theodore von Karman, Director of the Daniel Guggenheim Aeronautical Laboratory at the California Institute of Technology, Pasadena, California; and Glenn B. Woodruff, Consulting Engineer, of San Francisco."* Meeting dates: 28 Nov 1940 (Washington DC); 6–14 Dec 1940 (Seattle/Tacoma); 20–23 Jan 1941 (New York/Washington); 24–26 Feb 1941 (Pasadena); 17–28 Mar 1941 (Pasadena). *"Dr. von Karman directed the wind tunnel tests herein described (Appendix VIII)."*

Notable conclusions (verbatim, from the *Summary of Conclusions*): the bridge *"was well designed and built to resist safely all static forces… Its failure resulted from excessive oscillations caused by wind action"*; *"There is, however, no convincing evidence that the vertical oscillations were caused by so-called aerodynamic instability"*; *"The criteria usually considered for rigidity against static forces do not necessarily apply to dynamic forces."* **Crucially, this 1941 board had already rejected vortex resonance:** *"It is very improbable that **resonance** with alternating vortices plays an important role in the oscillations of suspension bridges. First, it was found that there is no sharp correlation between wind velocity and oscillation frequency such as is required in case of resonance with vortices whose frequency depends on the wind velocity."* It also found *"beyond a certain wind velocity negative aerodynamic damping is to be expected in almost any suspended bridge structure when it oscillates torsionally."*
Note the board's own causal finding differs from the modern aeroelastic-flutter account: it identified *"the first failure [as] the slipping of the cable band on the north side of the bridge to which the center ties were connected."*

So: **committee membership and the 26 June 1941 date and the $4,297,098.00 loss estimate are CONFIRMED** from the 1952 primary source.

### 3.5 When did wind-tunnel testing become standard / mandatory?

What I can verify:
- **"Common practice":** National Geographic (26 Nov 2025): *"The investigations and studies that followed reshaped national bridge-design standards. **Aerodynamic modeling and wind tunnel testing became common practice for modern suspension bridges.**"*
- **"Eventually became mandatory":** **[history.com, *Tacoma Bridge collapses*](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/tacoma-bridge-collapses)** (quoted by Wikipedia): *"After the Tacoma Narrows disaster, bridge builders took care to incorporate aerodynamics into their designs and build structures with complex frequencies. **Wind-tunnel testing of bridge designs eventually became mandatory.**"* This is a popular-history claim with no legal/standard citation. **I found no US federal regulation or ASCE standard, and no date, making wind-tunnel testing legally mandatory**, in any source I could retrieve.
- **"First use on a proposed bridge":** the NRHP nomination: *"It was the first time a research program was implemented to investigate the aerodynamic effects of wind acting upon a bridge. In designing this structure, **bridge engineers first used wind tunnel tests to determine the behavior and stability of a physical model of a proposed bridge**."*
- **FHWA involvement (verifiable):** the **Public Roads Administration** (a predecessor of FHWA) co-funded the 1941–45 UW model tests, paying roughly half of **$88,341.37** (WTBA Final Report). Today the **FHWA Turner-Fairbank Highway Research Center operates the Aerodynamics Laboratory**, described in **[FHWA-HRT-08-022, *Lab Clips*](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/general/08022/)** as *"the only wind tunnel facility in the United States dedicated solely to studying the complex interactions between wind and bridges or other highway structures."* (A more technical FHWA wind-research report exists at ROSA P, `rosap.ntl.bts.gov/view/dot/83157`, but it **returned 403** here.)

## 4. HOW THE COLLAPSE IS TAUGHT TODAY, AND THE 1990s/2000s CORRECTION

### 4.1 Billah & Scanlan (1991)

**Citation (verified via [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1119/1.16590)):** K. Yusuf Billah (Program in Structures and Mechanics, Princeton University) and Robert H. Scanlan (Department of Civil Engineering, Johns Hopkins University), *"Resonance, Tacoma Narrows bridge failure, and undergraduate physics textbooks,"* **American Journal of Physics 59(2), 118–124 (February 1991)**, DOI **10.1119/1.16590**.

**Exact central claim, verbatim from the published abstract** (retrieved from the [Mendeley catalogue record](https://www.mendeley.com/catalogue/b92aea54-2f9e-3a02-8c07-672ec99e2be7/)):

> "The dramatic Tacoma Narrows bridge disaster of 1940 is still very much in the public eye today. Notably, in many undergraduate physics texts the disaster is presented as an example of elementary forced resonance of a mechanical oscillator, with the wind providing an external periodic frequency that matched the natural structural frequency. This oversimplified explanation has existed in numerous texts for a long time and continues to this day, with even more detailed presentation in some new and updated texts. Engineers, on the other hand, have studied the phenomenon over the past half-century, and their current understanding differs fundamentally from the viewpoint expressed in most physics texts. In the present article the engineers' viewpoint is presented to the physics community to make it clear where substantial disagreement exists. First it is pointed out that one misleading identification of forced resonance arises from the notion that the periodic natural vortex shedding of the wind over the structure was the source of the damaging external excitation. It is then demonstrated that the ultimate failure of the bridge was in fact related to an **aerodynamically induced condition of self-excitation or ''negative damping'' in a torsional degree of freedom**. The aeroelastic phenomenon involved was an interactive one in which developed wind forces were strongly linked to structural motion. This paper emphasizes the fact that, **physically as well as mathematically, forced resonance and self-excitation are fundamentally different phenomena.** The paper closes with a quantitative assessment of the Tacoma Narrows phenomenon that is in full agreement with the documented action of both the bridge itself in its final moments and a full, dynamically scaled model of it studied in the 1950s."

Two further Billah–Scanlan wordings quoted via secondary sources:
- Their definition of resonance, quoted by Wikipedia (1940): *"In general, whenever a system capable of oscillation is acted on by a periodic series of impulses having a frequency equal to or nearly equal to one of the natural frequencies of the oscillation of the system, the system is set into oscillation with a relatively large amplitude."* — followed by: *"Could this be called a resonant phenomenon? It would appear not to contradict the qualitative definition of resonance quoted earlier, if we now identify the source of the periodic impulses as **self-induced**, the wind supplying the power, and the motion supplying the power-tapping mechanism. If one wishes to argue, however, that it was a case of **externally forced linear resonance**, the mathematical distinction … is quite clear, self-exciting systems differing strongly enough from ordinary linear resonant ones."*
- Their call to textbook authors, quoted by **[Vice](https://www.vice.com/en/article/the-myth-of-galloping-gertie/)**: *"While it is understandable how so many textbooks have, over the years, oversimplified the physics involved… it is probably time… to offer the next generation of students subtler, more complex and correct explanations."* (Vice notes: *"That was in 1991."*)

### 4.3 The AAPT DVD user's guide (2000) — verbatim

**Citation:** *Twin Views of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge Collapse — A user's guide to accompany the DVD*, **Copyright © 2000, American Association of Physics Teachers**, produced with the support of the AAPT Venture Fund at the University of Nebraska–Lincoln by **Robert G. Fuller, Charles R. Lang, and Roberta H. Lang**; ISBN 0-917853-95-4. Text retrieved from the AAPT's own PDF: **[aapt.org/Store/upload/tacoma_narrows2.pdf](https://aapt.org/Store/upload/tacoma_narrows2.pdf)** (28 pp., retrieved).

The passage on the cause, verbatim (from the section "The Physics of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge Collapse," by Robert G. Fuller and Dean A. Zollman):

> "As described in the 1991 paper by Billah and Scanlan, the motion of the bridge was an example of "an aerodynamically induced condition of self-excitation or negative damping in a torsional degree of freedom." **It was not a case of resonance, although that term is indeed erroneously used in one of the captions in the film first edited in 1962.** True, there are some features similar to resonance: a large periodic motion at one of the natural frequencies of oscillation of the structure, maintained by a relatively small continuously applied source of energy. **But true resonance requires the periodic application of a small driving force in synchronism with a natural frequency of the structure. The wind had no such frequency; it was a steady, rather strong wind of about 42 miles/hour.** Analysis dating back to the 1940s and 1950s shows that when a steady wind strikes a non-streamlined surface such as the side of the bridge deck, a "flutter wake" is produced which feeds energy back into the system. The energy tended to increase the motion of the structure. On the other hand, in all modes of transverse vibration, as the amplitude of vibration increased, so also did the damping factor, and the vibration was self-limited. Not so for the torsional modes: there was one particular torsional mode of vibration for which, at large amplitudes, the damping became negative. This one mode of torsional vibration was exceptional; the positive feedback from the flutter wakes caused the system's amplitude to increase steadily until structural failure occurred."

The guide also states the bridge history thus: *"The first Tacoma Narrows Bridge was built between November 23, 1938, and July 1, 1940, at a cost of approximately **$6,400,000**"*; total length 5,000 ft; span 2,800 ft; width (centre-to-centre) 39 ft, two lanes; side-girder height 8 ft; and *"The new bridge is four lanes wide and has open grid sides instead of solid I-beams. It was opened on October 14, 1950."*

### 4.5 The Vice article — "The Strangest, Most Spectacular Bridge Collapse (And How We Got It Wrong)"

**Citation:** Alex Pasternack, Vice, **14 December 2015**, **[vice.com/en/article/the-myth-of-galloping-gertie/](https://www.vice.com/en/article/the-myth-of-galloping-gertie/)** (retrieved full text). Its treatment of the resonance myth, verbatim:

> "…four decades after the central questions were answered—by Farquharson and his successors—**the common explanation taught in physics classrooms, resonance, is inaccurate.**"

> "For physics teachers, the footage of Gertie has proved irresistible as a lesson in wave motion—and, specifically, **a textbook example of forced resonance**. … **Scores of books and articles, from Encyclopedia Britannica to a Harvard course website, have reported that the Tacoma Narrows was destroyed by resonance.**"

> "After decades of research, physicists have congregated around a more specific explanation … That morning, as it had done for months, the bridge undulated in the wind, thanks to a set of aerodynamic forces that moved across the deck in a periodic oscillation—**but not at a resonant frequency** … The bridge's downfall was her own peculiar response to the wind, in a phenomenon known as **self-excitation, aeroelastic instability, or flutter**."

> "If resonance is a wine glass that's been shattered by an opera singer's voice as it vibrates at the glass' natural frequency, **self-excitation or flutter is more like an amp that shrieks when it gets too close to its own microphone.**"

> "**In mainstream and science media, however, that idea got drowned out by resonance.** The textbooks written by David Halliday and Robert Resnick in the early 1960s included photographs of the Tacoma Narrows Bridge in its section on resonance, and concluded that the 'wind produced a fluctuating resultant force in resonance with a natural frequency of the structure.' Bilah and Scanlan lay specific blame at the feet of Lee Edson, in his 1963 biography of Harman von Karman … 'The culprit in the Tacoma disaster was the Karman vortex Street,' Edson wrote, not quite correctly."

> "**In describing other examples of resonance, the Motherboard article regurgitated the fallacy that Galloping Gertie was wrecked by resonant frequencies. A correction has been issued.** But there are possibly thousands of other un-corrected articles and books, including in some from very large and seemingly reputable sources, including Encyclopedia Britannica and the Harvard math department."

The Vice article also reports the 1941 board finding (*"random action of turbulent wind"*), Farquharson's vortex-shedding de-correlation (*"the bridge would have shed vortices at about 1 hertz … out of synch with the .2 hertz torsional vibrations"*), the "28 feet on each side … 30 degrees" tilt estimate, and the footage-speed problem (see §4.6).

### 4.6 Related correction: the film footage is sped up

**[Physics Today, "The Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapse," Quick Study, 1 November 2015](https://physicstoday.aip.org/quick-study/the-tacoma-narrows-bridge-collapse-1760014552637)** — Donald W. Olson, Steven F. Wolf, Joseph M. Hook; DOI **10.1063/PT.3.2991** (retrieved). *"Some of what they saw was misleading."* The cameras ran at different speeds (Monroe at 24 fps, Elliott at 16 fps) and conversions assumed 24 fps throughout, so the on-bridge Elliott footage is roughly 40 % too fast: it shows *"about 18 cycles per minute, but Farquharson's own stopwatch that day measured a torsional frequency of 12 cycles per minute."* The AAPT DVD contains the same error (Vice: *"There is a crucial irony in the AAPT's paper. Its authors failed to note that the footage contained in the accompanying DVD is itself erroneous."*). The Physics Today piece also records: at **11:02 a.m.** a 600-ft section of roadway broke loose; about six minutes later almost all the remaining centre span dropped; the only casualty was the dog.

## 5. FATE OF THE WRECKAGE

### 5.1 Artificial reef

The collapsed superstructure became an artificial reef. [Wikipedia (1940)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacoma_Narrows_Bridge_(1940)): *"The portion of the bridge that fell into the water now serves as an artificial reef."* Best retrieved source: **[National Geographic, "How a doomed bridge became the Pacific Northwest's most unlikely reef," 26 November 2025](https://www.nationalgeographic.com/history/article/tacoma-narrows-bridge-collapse-artificial-reef)**: *"On the seafloor, the fallen bridge has become **one of the largest artificial reefs in the Pacific Northwest**"*, with remnants *"some more than 200 feet beneath the surface of the Puget Sound"*, quoting the Associated Press's phrase *"a barnacle-encrusted reef teeming with marine life."* Species reported by divers: giant Pacific octopus, wolf eels, plumose anemones, rockfish, kelp crabs. A 2021 documentary *"700 Feet Down"* covers the wreck.

### 5.2 National Register of Historic Places — reference number 92001068

**WHAT was listed:** the **"Tacoma Narrows Bridge Ruins"** — i.e. the **underwater remains** of the 1940 bridge, listed as **1 contributing site**, also known as "Galloping Gertie." **WHEN:** **added 31 August 1992.**

Sources: **[Wikipedia, *National Register of Historic Places listings in Pierce County, Washington*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Register_of_Historic_Places_listings_in_Pierce_County,_Washington)** (retrieved raw wikitext), row 86: `refnum=92001068`; `article=Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940)`; `name=Tacoma Narrows Bridge Ruins`; `address=State Route 16 over the Tacoma Narrows`; `date=1992-08-31`. Corroborated by **[HistoryPointer, NRIS 92001068](https://historypointer.com/nris/resource/92001068/)**: *"Added to the National Register of Historic Places **August 31, 1992**. NRIS 92001068. 1 contributing site. Also known as: Galloping Gertie."* Wikipedia (1940) states: *"The underwater remains of the highway deck of the old suspension bridge act as a large artificial reef, and these are listed on the National Register of Historic Places with reference number **92001068**."*

**Important disambiguation:** this is **not** the same listing as the 1950 replacement bridge. The **1950** bridge has its own **[NRHP nomination form](https://cms.cityoftacoma.org/planning/historic-preservation/NominationDocs/Narrows1950bridge.pdf)** ("Tacoma Narrows Bridge," Pierce County, WA), which carries **NRIS reference number 94001438** and is stamped **"NATIONAL REGISTER NOMINATION – RETURN"** — i.e. **the 1950-bridge nomination was returned to the preparer, not listed**, with the reviewer noting *"The case for 'exceptional' significance required for properties less than fifty years old has not been adequately substantiated."* Do not cite 94001438 as a listing, and do not attribute 92001068 to the standing bridge.

### 5.3 Salvage: exact figures

**Primary source: WTBA Final Report (1952), verbatim:**

> "The State realized a return of **$295,726.36** from salvage, the total cost of removal being **$646,661.83**, resulting in a **net cost of $350,935.47**. This amount is far below what the removal cost would have been had it been delayed until the contract for the new bridge was let due to increase in labor costs and decrease in scrap prices."

Contracts, verbatim: *"a cost plus contract was awarded the **J. H. Pomeroy and Company, Incorporated**, in December 1940 for removal of the balance of suspended structure remaining after failure. In August 1942 a contract was let to **J. Philip Murphy and Woodworth Company** for removal of cables and towers."* It adds: *"The removal of the cables was an extremely hazardous and difficult operation… It was the first time a suspension bridge cable of any magnitude had been removed without the use of catwalks."* And: *"because of the great need of metal and scrap in the war effort existing at the time, it was decided to salvage the steel and wire."*

Cross-check: Wikipedia (1940) says salvage *"continued into May 1943"* and reports *"a net loss of over $350,000"* — consistent with the primary figure of **$350,935.47**. The **HAER No. WA-99** record says the state *"collected **$4 million** in insurance compensation for the first bridge and salvage rights to its remains"*, and the AAPT guide gives the 1940 construction cost as *"approximately $6,400,000."*

### 5.4 Insurance: the settlement, and the "$5.2 million / 80 %" claim

**Primary source, WTBA Final Report (1952), verbatim:**

> "The original bridge was covered by insurance of the 'All Coverage Type' and by 'Use and Occupancy.' … **The State Board** consisted of the following members: **L. J. Sverdrup, Chairman; Francis Donaldson; Russel G. Cone**. **The Underwriters' Board** comprised the following: **Clifford E. Paine, Chairman; Hardy Cross; Shortridge Hardesty; Holton D. Robinson; Wilbur M. Wilson**. The report of the State Board was submitted on **June 26, 1941** and filed in Olympia. Their report of estimated loss was **$4,297,098.00**. The Underwriters' Board report was filed on **June 2, 1941** and their report of estimated loss was **$1,800,000.00**. … After studying the report, the insurance companies submitted an offer of **$4,000,000 plus salvage**, which was accepted by the State. **Use and Occupancy insurance in the amount of $240,000** was paid promptly by the carriers shortly after the failure. These amounts paid the bond holders in full with considerable funds remaining."

- **So the verified settlement is "$4,000,000 plus salvage," not a percentage of a stated value.**
- The claim that the bridge *"was insured by many other policies that covered **80 % of the $5.2 million** structure's value"* appears in **[Wikipedia, *Tacoma Narrows Bridge (1940)*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacoma_Narrows_Bridge_(1940))**, cited to a UW Special Collections exhibit page (`lib.washington.edu/specialcoll/exhibits/tnb/page5.html`) whose only accessible version is on **archive.org — unreachable here**, and whose modern host returned **403**. **I could not verify the $5.2 million value or the 80 % figure from any retrievable source, and the primary document records different numbers** ($4,000,000 + salvage; state board estimate $4,297,098.00; total cost incurred $6,618,138.53). Report the $5.2 M / 80 % claim as **unverified and in tension with the primary record.**
- Related fraud detail (Wikipedia, 1940): *"the State of Washington was unable to collect on one of the insurance policies for the bridge because its insurance agent had fraudulently pocketed the insurance premiums. The agent, **Hallett R. French**, who represented the Merchant's Fire Assurance Company, was charged and tried for grand larceny for withholding the premiums for **$800,000** worth of insurance."* Cited to a 22 May 1941 Roseburg, Oregon *News-Review* item via Newspapers.com — **not independently retrieved.**

### 5.5 The $6.4 million construction cost

- **Wikipedia (1940):** *"Construction took only nineteen months, at a cost of **$6.4 million**, which was financed by the grant from the PWA and a loan from the RFC."*
- **AAPT DVD user's guide (2000):** *"built between November 23, 1938, and July 1, 1940, at a cost of approximately **$6,400,000**."*
- **New York Times, 8 November 1940** (headline quoted by Wikipedia): *"the **$6,400,000** Tacoma Narrows Bridge collapsed with a roar today…"*
- **Primary source, WTBA Final Report:** total costs incurred as of 5 March 1941 = **$6,618,138.53**, financed by *"an outright grant of funds from the Public Works Administration … in the amount of **$2,894,958.86** and the proceeds from the sale of **$3,750,000** revenue bonds."* The low construction bid (27 Sept 1938, Pacific Bridge Company, with Bethlehem Steel as associate) was **$5,594,730.40**; PWA approved *"a grant of forty-five per cent of the cost."*
- **Reconciliation:** "$6.4 million" is a rounded construction-cost figure; the fully-accounted project cost including engineering, administration, interest during construction and interest on bonds was **$6,618,138.53**. Both should be given.
