81s REPORT primarily concerns the physi-
cal makeup, design, construction and costs of the Tacoma
Narrows Bridge. The present structure replaces an earlier
bridge at the same site which failed spectacularly from
vibrations induced by aerodynamic forces in the windstream.
Also, the present structure utilizes some portions of the
original bridge modified to fit the revised dimensions and
loadings. In order to more fully explain the conditions
facing the designers of the new bridge, a brief history of
the first Tacoma Narrows Bridge, its design and construc-
tion, failure and dismantling is included herein.

PART I
BRIEF HISTORY
OF
FIRST TACOMA NARROWS BRIDGE
For many years prior to the construction of the first Tacoma Narrows
Bridge, it had become quite evident that a crossing of The Narrows was a
public necessity in order to eliminate this formidable barrier with its deep,
swift tidal waters which separated Tacoma from the large undeveloped area
to the west and served only by an inadequate ferry system. Private interests
had attempted to promote and build a crossing on a location to the south of
the present bridge but were unsuccessful. The 1947 Legislature, recognizing
the need for this and other bridges, passed a law creating the Washington
Toll Bridge Authority with full powers to finance, construct and operate
toll bridges.
Immediately after the creation of the Authority, the Pierce County Com-
missioners requested the Authority to investigate and proceed with the build-
ing of such a bridge. An appropriation of $25,000 was made by the Legislature
to make a study of the problem. Such a study was completed on May 23, 1938
and on that date an application was submitted to the Public Works Administra-
tion requesting a grant of funds to assist in the design and construction of
such a structure.
Preliminary plans for the structure consisted, in brief, of a suspension
bridge with a central span of 2800 feet and side spans of 1100 feet each with
an open stiffening truss 25 feet in depth. The bridge provided a two lane road-
way and two sidewalks; deep piers of the cellular caisson type were used.
PW A had approved a grant of forty-five per cent of the cost but required
that the State employ a board of independent consultants to check plans. As
a result the firm of Moran & Proctor was employed for the substructure, and
Leon S. Moisseiff for the superstructure. This Board made a complete change
in both the superstructure and substructure, substituting an entirely different
type of foundation and using a shallow plate girder, eight feet in depth, for
the stiffening of the superstructure instead of the deep, open truss contemplated
by the State and shown on the grant application.
These amended plans were approved by PW A and bids asked to be
opened on September 2, 19.38. Only one bid was received. It was unopened
and was later learned to have been approximately $750,000 above the monies
available.
13
At this point, the Director of Highways called in the Consulting Board
which had been appointed on the Lake Washington Bridge, asking them to
review the plans upon which bids had been received and attempt to make
modifications which might reduce costs sufficiently to come within available
funds. This Board consisted of Chas. E. Andrew, Chairman; R. B. McMinn,
R.H. Thompson, and Luther E. Gregory.
The main piers, with some modifications, were changed back to the
cellular, bottom type first shown. Time did not permit a complete review of
the superstructure as the deadline for PW A aid expired on December 3 1,
1938; however, a change in the floor system and deck made a substantial saving.
The modified plans were approved by PW A and bids again opened on
September 27, 1938. The low bid in the amount of $5,594,730.40 was within
the funds available and was accepted. The Pacific Bridge Company was low
bidder; the Bethlehem Steel Company was an associate contractor who furn-
ished and erected the steel and wire. The bridge was opened to traffic on July
1, 1940.
During the construction period and prior to steel fabrication, evidence
of aerodynamic instability was reported on the Whitestone Bridge which was
of similar form and construction. The last appointed Board of Consultants
and the Director of Highways at once questioned the advisability of proceed-
ing with the bridge superstructure, as then designed, because of its even more
slender and lighter construction than that of the Whitestone Bridge. They
were assured, however, by the original designer and consultant, who was
considered to be the highest authority on suspension bridges, that a simple
means had been devised to correct the instability of the Whitestone Bridge
which could be applied, if necessary, to the Tacoma Narrows Bridge. Upon
such advice, construction proceeded according to plan; however, as soon as
floor forms were started, noticeable oscillation occurred. This oscillation steadily
increased while the bridge floor was placed, varying in intensity in accordance
with wind conditions. Finally on November 7, 1940, the superstructure failed
and a large part of the bridge floor and stiffening girders fell into the Sound
leaving the cables and towers with the side span floor system still standing.

entirely completed at the time of failure, is shown in the following statement
of costs.

REMOVAL OF REMAINING PORTIONS OF THE STRUCTURE
As previously stated, the piers, anchorages, rowers, side span floor systems
and west approach remained standing after the failure. The anchorages, piers
and the greater part of the west approach were unharmed. The towers and
cables were damaged co such an extent that they had no value except for
salvage and at the same time constituted a menace to navigation. It, therefore,
16
became necessary to remove the towers and cables. This could have been ac-
complished by simply cutting the strands at the anchorages and allowing them
to fall into the sound, the water being of such great depth that no menace to
navigation would have remained. However, because of the great need of metal
and scrap in the war effort existing at the time, it was decided to salvage the
steel and wire. To accomplish this end, a cost plus contract was awarded the
]. H. Pomeroy and Company, Incorporated, in December 1940 for removal
of the balance of suspended structure remaining after failure. In August 1942
a contract was let to J. Philip Murphy and Woodworth Company for removal
of cables and towers.
The removal of the cables was an extremely hazardous and difficult op-
eration requiring ingenuity on the part of the engineers and contractor. It was
the first time a suspension bridge cable of any magnitude had been removed
without the use of catwalks. The dismantling was accomplished without the
loss of life or major injury.
The State realized a return of $295,726.36 from salvage, the total cost
of removal being $646,661.83, resulting in a net cost of $350,935.47. This
amount is far below what the removal cost would have been had it been delayed
until the contract for the new bridge was let due to increase in labor costs and
decrease in scrap prices.

PART Ill
DECISION TO REBUILD THE BRIDGE
There was a great increase in traffic over the former bridge, as compared
with that which had used the ferries. This increase averaged 145 % during the
four months of operation and con.firmed the public necessity for a bridge over
The Narrows. As a result the Toll Bridge Authority by resolution in June 1941
requested the Director of Highways to determine whether or not it was pos-
sible and feasible to rebuild the crossing with a bridge which would be safe
and .financially sound.
DESIGN AND RESEARCH ON NEW STRUCTURE
Engineers in charge realized they were confronted with a new and dif-
ferent problem which was without precedent. The question of aerodynamic
stability in bridge design had not been investigated. In order to pursue such
an investigation, a Consulting Board was appointed consisting of Sverdrup &
Parcel of St. Louis and Glenn B. Woodruff of San Francisco, as members, with
Chas. E. Andrew as Chairman. Dr. Theodor von Karman of the California
Institute of Technology was appointed as special aerodynamic consultant. The
Board's first meeting was held in the fall of 1941.
In the meantime, a design department was formed under the direction
of Chas. E. Andrew. Tentative plans were developed based upon all the avail-
able information in the field of aerodynamics, most of which applied to the
design of airplanes. It soon became quite evident that the science as applied to
airplanes, which are moving objects not in fixed position and fully susceptible
to streamlining, was quite different than that which could be applied to a fixed
blunt object such as a bridge. Only fundamental considerations could be used.
Certain aerodynamic laws were diametrically opposite. It became necessary,
beyond a certain point, to develop new theories and techniques. The problem,
therefore, became a question of original research. The outstanding development
of the airplane in recent years had been accomplished by a more or less "cut
. and try" method. A plane was designed and flown; if it did not meet require-
ments its design was changed and flight repeated until a satisfactory result was
obtained. In more recent years the process was simplified and cheapened by the
use of scaled models tested in artificial wind streams developed by wind tunnels.
It seemed logical to follow the latter process in the design of the bridge.
A wind tunnel sufficiently large to test a 1 to 50 scale model was designed and
built at the University of Washington. As bridge designs were developed they
were tested in the tunnel. Many changes in design were necessary before the
desired degree of stability was obtained by test.
18
The design of the bridge and original research transpired wholly within
the period of the second World War and were greatly handicapped by lack of
personnel and difficulty in obtaining necessary materials with the result that
approximately four years were spent in the process. We are, however, con-
fident that the results fully justified the effort. This conclusion has been verified
by the fact that the bridge, as now constructed, withstood wind velocities on
several occasions during January, February and March of 1951 as high as 75
miles per hour without any visible sign of vertical or torsional oscillation.
Several unique details of design developed in these studies were used in the
final plans. The great volume of design studies and test data would require
too much space to recount in this report. Details of the final design are shown
on the contract plans and a full account of model tests is given in reports now
being compiled at the University of Washington where the tests were made.
All designs were made in the design department, organized for that pur-
pose in Tacoma, and model tests of these designs were conducted in a specially
constructed wind tunnel at the University of Washington with some check
tests being made at the California Institute of Technology at Pasadena, Cali-
fornia. The cost of model tests was financed jointly by the Washington Toll
Bridge Authority and the Public Roads Administration; each public bcdy
assuming approximately one-half the cost which amounted to $88,341.37.
Design of the New Structure
(a) Fo1tndt1tions and To'll'ers. The failure of the former bridge caused
co damage to the main piers and anchorages and only minor damage to the
west approach structure. Since these portions of the bridge had a value of about
$3,000,000, it was quite necessary they should be utilized, if possible, in the
design for the new structure. The main piers were carefully examined by divers
both inside and out with no evidence of injury being found.
The former bridge, a two lane structure, was designed with tower legs
39 feet apart at their tops battering to 50 foot centers at their bases. Towers
for the new structure of four lanes, requiring a cable spacing of 60 feet, were
designed with vertical legs. This required the former tower pedestals to be
removed and new pedestals constructed at 60 foot centers. The piers, as orginally
constructed, provided ample room for this purpose; in fact, shifting the tower
pedestals resulted in a better distribution of load to the main walls of the piers
than was previously achieved.
The question then arose as to whether or not the added load of a four
lane bridge would cause excessive foundation pressures. Piers of this type and
depth ( 225 feet for Pier 5 and 17 5 feet for Pier .:{ below mean low water) arc:
many times heavier in actual buoyant weight than the superstructure which
19
they support. In addition to being designed for stability under full load, they
must be able to resist stress during construction when towers are "free-standing"
and such other factors as ship collision and tidal forces. It was found in this
case that the actual dead load foundation pressures were increased only ap-
proximately six per cent by the added weight of the new superstructure.
Since the foundation material consists of clean sand, gravel and boulders
in which the piers were deeply embedded, an increase of six per cent was con-
sidered of little consequence. The dead load pressure, as the bridge now stands,
does not exceed 15,300 lbs./sq. ft.
Seventeen recorded earthquakes had occurred in this area since the original
piers were constructed in 1939. Five of these quakes were of 5 or greater in-
rensity measured on the Mercalli Scale, while two in 1946 were rated 7. In
addition to these earthquakes, the destruction of the original bridge undoubtedly
imposed lateral forces and pressures on the foundation material of a much
greater intensity than will likely occur under the new structure. Very accurate
surveys since the failure have indicated no movement whatsoever, lateral or
vertical, occurred in the main piers.
After due consideration of all the factors involved, the Board of Consulting
Engineers decided that no modification of the existing channel piers was neces-
sary below the top slab.
An independent study of these piers was made in 1946 and 1947 by F. M.
Masters of the consulting firm of Modjeski and Masters, Harrisburg, Pennsyl-
vania. Their report dated January 10, 1947 substantiated the conclusions of the
Consulting Board.
Soundings taken around the channel piers during 1940 and later in 1946
indicated that some scour had occurred, principally at Pier 4 ( the west pier).
Class "C" riprap was included in the construction contract for the future pro-
tection of the piers against such scour.
As previously stated, design of the new bridge involved removal of the
old ped.~stals and construction of new pedestals at 60 foot centers. The former
pedestals were constructed to elevation +23.1 feet which was 15.6 feet above
high tide. This had proven too low an elevation as salt water spray had started
serious corrosion at the base of the old towers. The new pedestals were designed
to the elevation of +40.0 feet raising the base of the new tower steel 32.5
feet above maximum high tide.
An interesting innovation was developed in the design of these pedestals
to secure a true level plane surface on their tops. Such a surface is necessary
for setting of base slabs and insuring verticality of rowers. To obtain such a
plane ( approximately 20 fr. hy 10 ft.) previous similar construction required
20
considerable grinding and finishing of the final surface, sometimes involving
several weeks of delicate work. The following method was devised and employed
for the constn1ction of these pedestals:
Before pouring the upper portion of pedestals, a structural steel frame
was set up from elevation 26.0 to approximately 12 inches below the finished
surface. The upper portion of pedestals was then poured with the exception of
12 inches under the surface on which tower base plates were to be set. Adjusting
screws and screed bars were then placed and attached to structural frame. Screed
bars were 4 x ½ inch steel bars machined finished on top edge to 0.008 inches
from a straight line. Screed bars were placed on approximately 20 inch centers
forming a checkerboard pattern over the entire surface to be finished. Adjusting
links provided with 8 and 12 pitch stock provided very accurate setting of the
finished edge of screed bars.
Specifications provided for pouring Class "A" concrete in this area to a
plane ½ inch below top of screeds. A layer of 1 to 2 mortar was then placed
and struck off 1 / 16 inch above the screeds. This mortar surfa~e was allowed
to harden about six hours when the entire surface was dressed down by steel
straight edges to the elevation of the steel screeds. It might be mentioned here
that the above method proved very satisfactory in obtaining a perfectly true
surface.
Very little modification was necessary on the other existing piers. Ap-
proximately 500 cu. yds. of concrete was added to Pier No. 3 at the westerly
end of the suspended structure to provide the additional weight required for
the cable holddowns. At Pier No. 6, the easterly end of the suspended structure,
it was necessary to widen the body of the pier in addition to adding weight for
holddowns. Pjers Nos. 1 and 2, under the existing west approach structure,
required no modification.
The anchorages, while uninjured by the collapse of the former bridge,
had relatively less value than the piers for the following reasons:
1. They were of insufficient weight to resist the horizontal pull of the
new bridge cables.
2. The embedded eyebars were at 39 foot centers whereas the new bridge
cables would require them to be 60 feet apart.
It therefore became necessary to remove a large yardage of concrete from
each side of the blocks to provide room for embedment of the new eyebars
and utilize the weight of the remaining concrete. Consequently, a considerable
amount of the anchorages became a liability rather than an asset.
21
Both anchorages are of the gravity type depending wholly upon friction
to resist the horizontal pull of the cables. To supply the additional weight re-
quired for the new bridge cables and provide space for the new eyebar anchors,
it was first necessary to remove approximately 11,000 cubic yards of concrete
from the sides of the anchorages by drilling and shooting down to the top of
the main foundation blocks. These foundation blocks were then widened eight
feet outward on each side of the anchorage to provide additional width for
the new anchor bars. The foundation block and body of anchorage were further
enlarged by adding 20 feet to the back for the full height and width with the
result that after the new anchor bars were set and embedded in concrete, a
complete .. horseshoe" of new concrete encompassed the old concrete core.
The new concrete was designed to be poured in alternating blocks of 150 to
220 cubic yards to relieve shrinkage stresses as much as possible.
The anchorages are designed for a coefficient of friction against cable pull
of approximately 0.34, their respective weights being in the order of 54,000
tons each.
An interesting problem arose at Piers 4 and 5 ( the main channel piers)
in making provision for tower erection. The maximum bending stresses in the
towers occur during erection when they are .. free standing" and subject to
maximum wind load and other lateral forces. The usual method for resisting
free standing tower moments is through use of structural anchors and anchor
bolts deeply embedded in the concrete. To develop new anchors of this type, it
would have been necessary to modify the entire top of the piers well below
water line in order to engage the necessary weight. Such a procedure would have
been extremely expensive and of little value after completion of the bridge.
As designed, the original structural steel anchors, even though eccentric
with the new towers, were preserved as far as practical during removal of the
old pedestals. The new pedestals were enlarged sufficiently to encompass them
and additional overturning resistance provided by a system of structural steel
outriggers which in effect greatly increased the width of tower bases.
This arrangement proved to be entirely successful as evidenced by the
fact that the east tower was free standing at its full height of 462 feet when
the most severe recorded earthquake in the area occurred on April 13, 1949.
The tower suffered no damage during the earthquake although one cable saddle,
which had been temporarily blocked on to its top, was thrown clear of the pier.
The outriggers were of course temporary and were removed after cable
spinning and a certain stage of superstructure erection had been reached. Pic-
tures of the arrangement are shown in appendix.
Tower No. 3 of the original bridge, at the west end of the suspended
structure, was undamaged by the collapse. It also served as a support for the

that approximately $200,000 could be saved by incorporating these portions
of the old bridge in the new structure.
In order to accomplish this, one new intermediate strut was added to
Tower 3 approximately 100 feet from its base to stiffen and strenghthen the

The original west approach structure was a through plate girder, con-
tinuous over three 150 foot spans between Tower 3 and the anchorage. Girders
were on 4 3 foot centers and accommodated two traffic lanes with 7 foot side-
walks. Roadway and sidewalk slabs were of standard hard rock concrete.
This approach structure was redesigned to accommodate four traffic lanes
by raising the floorbeams and stringers to the top of main girders and in effect
redesigning it to a deck plate girder structure. The use of lightweight concrete
slab reduced the dead load sufficiently so that the structure was capable of
carrying four lanes of live load. Sidewalk brackets and stringers were added
outside of the main girders.
Damage to the original approach structure was principally confined to
the ends of several floorbeams where the cables had injured them during col-
lapse of main span. Damaged portions of these floorbeams were cut off and
new material spliced on during reconstruction.
The original east approach structure extending for 210 feet between Pier
6 and the east anchorage was of reinforced concrete. It had suffered little dam-
age during collapse but of course could not be re-used to fit new grades and
a four lane roadway. It was therefor entirely removed and a new reinforced
concrete structure designed to fit the four lane structure and merge into the
six traffic lanes at the toll gates.
23
In designing the suspended spans for the new bridge it was felt that every
possible precaution should be taken to insure against any possibility of notice-
able motion occurring in the structure. Such a design would not only insure the
.financial success. of the project but also serve to re-establish public confidence
in major bridge construction and the engineering profession in general which
undoubtedly had suffered due to collapse of the former bridge.
One of the main contributing factors causing collapse of the former
bridge was its extreme flexibility. In the final design for the new bridge, the
stiffening trusses are on 60 foot centers with a depth of 3 3 feet center to center
of chords. Trusses of the new bridge have an average moment of inertia of
6,890,000 ins. 4 as compared to 120,400 ins. 4 for the original bridge. This fol-
lows what might be called the conservative practice of suspension bridge design
in providing maximum stiffness and depth within live load stress limitations.
All parts of the suspended structure were designed with the view of re-
ducing areas exposed to the wind as much as possible. This is especially notice-
able in handrail, curbs and floorbeams. Floorbeams are trussed type, principally
composed of six-inch beam sections with a depth of eleven feet. A considerable
saving in steel was also made through the use of this type floorbeam instead
of the conventional girder type floorbeam.
Another departure from usual past practice was the use of a double lateral
system of bracing in the planes of the top and bottom chords. This greatly in-
creased the torsional rigidity of the suspended structure.
An entirely new innovation in suspension bridge design was introduced
through the use of open gratings as longitudinal slots at curb lines and between
roadway lanes. The curb slots are nineteen inches in width and the roadway
slots are thirty-three inches wide separating the four concrete slab traffic lanes
of nine foot width. The slots were designed to be fabricated from five-inch
I-Beam-Lok open floor grating with six-inch channel edges. The main mem-
bers of the grating were cut to extend two inches through holes punched in
upper and lower parts of the channel edge beams. Hoop-shaped anchor straps
were welded to the prongs and extended approximately twelve inches into the
concrete slab. These served effectively to splice the open grating to the rein-
forced concrete slab and caused the slab to act continuously over all stringers.
Because of the rather radical departure of the above roadway floor from
conventional design, a section of such a slab was constructed on similarly spaced
stringers with strain gages attached to reinforcing bars, anchor straps and mem-
bers of slotted gratings. Various positions of wheel loads were tested and, even
with overloading to approximately 13 5 ';r, comparatively low steel stresses
were recorded with no signs of cracking in concrete at any point. The low
24
stresses recorded under these loadings were probably due to the channel edge
beams of open gratings serving as distributing beams or, in other words, serv-
ing to transfer superimposed loads over a greater area of slab than would be
possible under ordinary reinforced concrete slab construction.
Extensive studies were made of various slotted deck designs in the wind
tunnel at the University of Washington. During the course of these investi-
gations it soon became apparent that such a deck possessed inherent charac-
teristics of great value in stabilizing the structure. The final deck design used
represented what appeared co be the proper proportions best suited for the
structure. A completely open-grated deck for such a long structure was con-
sidered objectionable for several reasons.
The curb slots were designed with integral curb. Supports of trimmed
seven-inch tee-sections spaced at approximately five foot centers were welded
to the top of main members of the grating. These in turn supported a five-
inch standard pipe curb centered twelve inches above the surface of roadway
and welded to supports. The resulting curb presents a very sturdy appearance
offering little resistance to wind.
A secondary benefit derived from use of slotted deck design is its effec-
tiveness as traffic separators or lane markers. An errant driver's attention is
immediately alerted if he leaves his lane and his wheels cross onto the grating
of the slots.
The design for the suspended structure also includes external energy ab-
sorbing devices consisting of oil-filled hydraulic cylinders at the towers and
as diagonal ties between the trusses and cable at the center of the main span.
They are designed to act as dampening devices under the comparative rapid
motion of oscillation while offering little resistance to the slower motion or
deflections occuring from live loads or temperature changes.
( c) C1bles. In the design of a suspension bridge the various parts of the
structure are rather closely inter-related. The dead load of the suspended struc-
ture and its resistance to wind pressure is immediately transferred to the cable
which supports it and, in turn, to the rowers, piers and anchorages supporting
the cable. Thus any saving of weight or exposed area in the suspended struc-
ture reflects savings in design for other parts of the structure.
For this reason it was decided to use a lightweight aggregate concrete
for the roadway and sidewalk slabs on the suspended structure. Specifications
called for a design mix of three-quarter inch maximum coarse, intermediate
and fine lightweight aggregates with the addition of natural sand and eight
sacks of cement per cubic yard to produce a concrete of not to exceed 120
pounds per cubic foot and provide a compressive strength of not less than
25
thirty-six hundred pounds per square inch at 28 days. Actual design mix av-
eraged about 118 lbs/cu.ft. in weight, and test cylinders showed a minimum
compressive strength of 4500 lbs/sq.in. after 28 days.
In order to further reduce the size of main cables and keep the pull on the
anchorages to a minimum, a sag ratio of 1 : 10 for main span was adopted as
compared to 1: 12 for the former bridge. While this necessitated an increase
in height of towers it reflected considerable saving in cost of anchorages and
cables.
The main cables are composed of nineteen strands of four hundred fifty-
eight ( 458) No. 6 gage wires providing an area of two hundred fifty-two
( 2 5 2) square inches of steel. Design stresses in cable wire were 70,000 lbs.
per square inch due to dead load alone or 78,000 lbs. per square inch for com-
bined loading. Specifications required a minimum tensile strength of 220,000
lbs. per square inch of gross section with a yield point of 150,000 lbs. per
square inch. The final compressed cable was computed to have a diameter of
twenty and one-eighth ( 20 Vs ) inches.
Anchor bars and adjusting links were designed of structural carbon steel
with welded pin plates. Specifications provided for annealing of bars after
welding. Anchor bars and strands splayed from a theoretical point approxi-
mately 28 feet vertically in 135 feet and 16 feet horizontally in 140 feet. The
bars and adjusting links extend approximately sixty-two feet into the main
body of the anchorages.
The strand shoes were also designed of structural carbon steel. They are
three and seven-eighths inches thick and grooved to a depth of one and three-
fourths inches to provide a minimum inside diameter of twenty-six inches around
which the main cable wires are looped.
Cable saddles at the towers are structural carbon steel of a welded web-
type design. The curved cable trough is constructed to a center line cable radius
of thirteen feet. The curved base of the cable saddle as well as the upper cable
retainer castings are finished in grooves to fit the contour of the lower and
upper strands as they lie in the saddle. Zinc fillers approximately six inches
long strung on one-fourth inch wire rope fill the interstices between the strands
through the saddle.
Cable bands as originally designed were to consist of cast steel hanger
saddles welded to structural steel bands. This was later revised and the entire
bands were made of cast steel with interior surfaces given a medium rough
finish.
The stiffening truss is supported at each interior panel point by four 1.Ys
inch hanger ropes except at the center jacks where I !Ii inch ropes are used.
26
Holddowns at Tower No. 3 are composed of eight 2Ys inch ropes and at Pier
No. 6 are composed of eight I¾ inch ropes. The holddown bands are designed
in two sections with ring connections to provide for slight angle change in
main cable. Suspender ropes were pre-stressed to fifty per cent of their ultimate
strength.
No. 9 annealed double galvanized steel wire with an ultimate strength
of 85,000 psi was used for wrapping the main cables. Wrapping was applied
over red lead paste with a minimum wire tension at approximately 450 pounds.
Cable band bolts were carbon manganese high tensile steel with a mini-
mum tensile strength requirement of 110,000 psi.
One-inch handropes with vertical stanchions at each cable band were
placed on the cables and extend for the full length of stiffening trusses.

GENERAL STATEMENT COVERING CONSTRUCTION
SPECIAL EVENTS AND SAFETY REGULATIONS
Construction was started promptly after contracts were awarded. The
contract a wards were delayed, however, for a period of ninety days after opening
of bids due to difficulties encountered in obtaining insurance and completing
financial arrangements. This delay made it necessary for the State to request
an extension of the limiting date of award after bid opening from the Con-
tractors and to some extent upset the construction time schedule as originally
planned; the most serious result being the advancing of cable spinning from
a favorable to an extremely unfavorable period of weather.
All concrete work, including anchorage enlargements, pier modifications,
concrete approaches and bridge floor ,vas performed in a very efficient and
satisfactory manner by Woodworth & Company under subcontract from Beth-
lehem Pacific Coast Steel Corporation. The only unusual type of work in this
connection was the removal of some 12,000 cubic yards of concrete from the
existing anchorages of the former bridge to make way for the placing of new
anchor bars and enlargement of the anchorages to sustain the increased hori-
zontal pull of the new and heavier bridge cables; likewise, the removal of tower
pedestals from the main piers and construction of new ones to accommodate
the wider and heavier towers required.
The original design of the concrete floor slabs did not specify the use of
a wearing surface other than the concrete itself. Subsequent study of the abra-
sive qualities of lightweight concrete led to the use, in final construction, of
an asphaltic wearing surface. The thickness of this wearing surface was limited
by conditions of the original design to ~/8 of an inch. Had these restrictions not
been present, a somewhat greater thickness would have been used. However,
after one and one-half years usage under heavy truck traffic no sign of edge
raveling or loosing is evident, due probably to the fact that all edges are steel
armored and to the careful bonding co the concrete surface.
Formation of true planes for tower bases was simplified to a considerable
extent over the conventional method of grinding by use of the steel grid de-
scribed under "Design." Tower erection was accomplished by means of the
conventional "crawler crane" method.
An excellent job of fabrication was done by the Bethlehem Pacific Coast
Steel Corporation on the towers with the result that when the towers were
erected to full height their length was within a few thousandths of design di-
mension. This result was greatly expedited by the fact that specifications re-
quired all tower splice plates be removed in the placing of tower cells using
only the corner angles as guides for position. In this manner all dire and scale
could be removed prior to contact and all tower joints brought to true bearing
quickly and exactly with all rivet holes fair.
No tower top planing was necessary as all inequalities from a true plane
could be made up by means of thin stainless steel shims.
On April 13, 1949, during the erection of Tower 5, an unusual incident
occurred. Both tower legs were erected to full height with the exception of the
top cross struts. Just before noon of that day the most severe earthquake of
modern times in the northwest occurred reaching an acceleration of 8 % of
gravity.
The contractor had lifted the cable saddle to the top of the south leg
and had blocked it on timber cribbing about four feet above the tower top some
510 feet above the water level. The free standing tower was of course subject
to a considerable amplitude in vibration which dislodged the saddle weighing
about twenty-one tons and pitched it into the Sound in approximately 140 feet
of water. In its descent the saddle crashed through a large work barge anchored
to the south end of the pier sinking it and losing a considerable amount of
contractor's equipment. Fortunately no fatalities occurred and only two men
were slightly injured.
The saddle was subsequently recovered by divers and after straightening
a small corner bend in the base was again placed on the tower. No damage
was done to the tower.
On June 7, 1949, a fire occurred at Pier 4 during tower erection. The
fire destroyed the creosoted timber fender beyond repair with only slight dam-
age to the tower steel, buckling a few plates near the base and blistering some
paint areas. However, the contractor suffered considerable loss in equipment.
Lacer the fender was completely rebuilt, the loss being partially covered
by insurance.
Stiffening truss erection was accomplished by placing individual mem-
bers rather than by lifting assembled sections from the water as in the original
bridge. Four rolling derricks were used, starting each way from the towers.
As the erection advanced, materials and members were delivered over rail
track from the towers to each of the derricks. Member connections were made
as holes became fair. No unusual event occurred during erection of the trusses.
Cable spinning was seriously delayed by the severe weather conditions
which existed during the fall and winter 1949-1950. Almost continuous rain
and snow fell for a period of six weeks accompanied hy considerable wind.
As spinning advanced, when weather permitted, the cables in their unsqueezed
condition hecarne fulJ of ice and snow \Vith the result that the squeezing and
30
wrapping could not be accomplished until the cables thawed out. In all, the
contractor was delayed as much as sixty days in the completion from this cause
which was beyond his control.
The bridge was opened to traffic on October 14, 1950, although paint-
ing was not finished until October 1951 due to the fact that paint could not
be successfully applied during winter weather.
Safety regulations were under the direction of the Department of Labor
and Industries, and supervised directly by J. F. Burns, State Safety Inspeaor.
While fatal accidents are always regrettable, they seem almost impos-
sible to avoid in major construction. Four men lost their lives during the
construction period: Two by falling, one by being hit under a falling derrick
boom and one undoubtedly by heart attack. All things considered, this is
better than the average accident record. Comparatively few accidents result-
ing in non-fatal bodily injury occurred.

PART IV
BEHAVIOR OF STRUCTURE
SINCE OPENING TO TRAFFIC
In the period preceding opening of the bridge ro traffic, considerable
vibration was noted in the long hanger ropes adjacent to rowers during period
of high winds. On the longer hangers which approached 300 feet in length,
and during the higher winds, the ropes moved ro such an extent as ro slap
against one another near the centers.
In an effort ro remedy chis situation, hanger spreaders were installed at
the approximate midpoints of the hangers and extending out twelve panels
each way from the cowers. These spreaders were constructed of cast steel in
the form of an "X" ,vith a cap bolted co each prong which gripped the hanger
ropes in a sheer of lead filler material.
After opening, the ,vinter of 1950-5 l subjected the structure ro some
of the highest winds that have been experienced in this area for a number
of years. During this period it was found that additional spreaders were needed
to control the vibrations and 150 more were installed. These were located
parallel to the roadway approximately fifty feet above the rop chord of the
truss and extended 18 ro 19 panels each way from the rowers.
Since the latter installation was made only slight, slow movements have
been observed. These only occur on the longest hangers when the group of
four ropes move as a unit under very high winds.
The hanger vibrations described had not been considered as creating
any particularly dangerous situation since even when the most severe vibra-
tions occurred no morion or bending of ropes was apparent near the collars
on the chords and cables. However, because of the great public interest in
the bridge and the sad fate of its predecessor, it was felt chat any such move-
ment which was at all noticeable to the public should be eliminated.
During these periods of high winds since opening of the bridge, lateral
deflection estimated at from three and one-half ro four feet has occurred in
che main span. Velocity of winds during this period has been estimated at 65
to 70 mph. However, at no time has any vibratory motion in the structure been
apparent.
After each period of high winds, public reaction was typical and many
inquiries were received as to the behavior of the bridge. The fact that no
motion was experienced has served co re-establish public confidence in this
bridge and probably ro some extent will exert a favorable reaction rowards
construction of ocher suspension bridges in the future.
